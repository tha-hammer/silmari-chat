# AIDA Framework

> The fundamental structure of all persuasive copy. AIDA is fractal — it applies at the sentence, paragraph, section, and full-piece level simultaneously.

---

## The Core Model

**A — Attention** → Stop the scroll. Interrupt the pattern. Get them to pay attention.
**I — Interest** → Hook them into caring. Show you understand their world.
**D — Desire** → Build want. Paint the vision. Stack proof. Make them lean in.
**A — Action** → Remove friction. Give them a clear, compelling next step.

---

## Fractal Application

AIDA doesn't just apply to the overall piece — it applies at every level:

| Level | Attention | Interest | Desire | Action |
|-------|-----------|---------|--------|--------|
| **Individual sentence** | First word hooks | Middle develops | End intensifies | Leads to next sentence |
| **Paragraph** | Opening sentence | Body sentences | Closing emotion | Reader continues |
| **Section** | Section headline | Opening paragraphs | Core argument | Section CTA or bridge |
| **Full piece** | Headline/opening | Problem + mechanism | Proof + vision | Final CTA |

**The rule:** If a paragraph doesn't contain micro-AIDA, it's a weak paragraph. Rewrite it.

## Emotional-Logical Ratios

Each AIDA component has an optimal emotional-to-logical ratio:

| Component | Emotional | Logical | Reasoning |
|-----------|-----------|---------|-----------|
| **Attention (A)** | 80% | 20% | Emotion gets attention; logic sets up the why |
| **Interest (I)** | 60% | 40% | Emotional hook must be supported by credible setup |
| **Desire (D)** | 40% | 60% | Logic justifies what emotion already wants |
| **Action (Ac)** | 90% | 10% | Urgency is almost entirely emotional; logic reassures |

**The principle:** People decide emotionally and justify logically. Lead with emotion, follow with logic.

## Pain-Centric Copy (The 80/20 Rule)

The most important ratio in sales copy:
- **80% of the copy**: Focus on the prospect's pain — the gap between their current state and desired state
- **20% of the copy**: The solution and offer

**Why:** People are more motivated by pain avoidance than by pleasure seeking. Spend most of the copy in the pain — making it vivid, specific, and felt — before offering the way out.

**Pain framing guidelines:**
- Never use pain manipulatively — use it to connect on a deeper level
- Paint the emotional pictures: "drowning in despair," "trapped in a cage," "lost in a dark forest"
- Use "trigger moments" — specific incidents that crystallize the problem (the daughter who said "mommy you're squishy")
- Only near the end does the solution appear as "the only way out"

## Application by Content Type

### Short-Form (LinkedIn, social posts)
- Attention: 1-2 sentences maximum
- Interest: 2-3 sentences
- Desire: 1-2 sentences (one proof point)
- Action: 1 clear sentence

### Medium-Form (Sales messages, emails)
- Attention: Opening paragraph
- Interest: 2-3 paragraphs (problem + story)
- Desire: 3-4 paragraphs (solution + proof + vision)
- Action: 1-2 paragraphs (CTA + urgency)

### Long-Form (Sales pages, blog articles)
- Attention: Headline + subheadline + opening hook (can be 200-500 words)
- Interest: Full problem exploration (20-30% of piece)
- Desire: Full solution build with all proof types (40-50% of piece)
- Action: Close with vision + urgency + CTA (15-20% of piece)

## Awareness Levels (Eugene Schwartz)

Match the headline and opening to the prospect's awareness level:

| Awareness Level | They Know | How to Open |
|----------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Product-aware** | They know your product | Lead with the product |
| **Solution-aware** | They know solutions exist, not yours | Lead with the desire/outcome |
| **Problem-aware** | They know they have a problem, not the solution | Lead with the problem |
| **Symptom-aware** | They feel pain but can't name it | Lead with the symptom, crystallize it |
| **Unaware** | Don't know they have a problem | Lead with a story or big idea |

## Common AIDA Failure Modes

**Failing at Attention:**
- Opening with company/product name (no one cares yet)
- Generic promise ("improve your business")
- Passive voice and corporate speak

**Failing at Interest:**
- Jumping to features before establishing pain
- Talking about yourself instead of the prospect
- Boring, predictable second sentences

**Failing at Desire:**
- Listing features without translating to benefits
- Generic testimonials ("I loved it!")
- No specificity in results claims

**Failing at Action:**
- Multiple competing CTAs
- Vague CTA ("learn more")
- No urgency or reason to act now
